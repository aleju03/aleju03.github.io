# src/game

Guidance that only applies inside the game runtime, so it loads only when you work here. The repo-wide rules (the layer cake, the collision contract, the far-plane rule, the React-free/renderer-optional rule) stay in the root `CLAUDE.md` and are still in context. `src/game/README.md` is the long-form map and how-to-add-things guide; this file is the short list of things that will bite you.

## The two subsystems this file covers

- `src/game/world/` is **the open world**: an endless, chunk-streamed procedural planet with Earth-like biomes, water and walkable cities. Everything is a pure function of (x, z), hashed by position and never streamed, so a chunk rebuilds identically whichever direction you arrive from. `houseWorld.ts` still owns the property line inward and the generator builds nothing on that rectangle (`grid.ts`'s `RESERVED`) but its ground, drawn two centimetres under the house's floors so the yard is the same turf as the verge past the fence; the world grid is offset so chunk (0,0) is the house's block and the generated street lands exactly where the old hand-made one was. The three numbers that place the house on the planet (`land.ts`'s `WORLD_X/Z` and `CLIMATE_X/Z`) were calibrated by probing the fields, not guessed. Move them and re-probe rather than hunting for a kind seed. `README.md`'s "The open world" section is the guide. It also owns what *moves* out there but isn't part of the ground, and all three of those are session state like the fleet, built and ticked from `outsideWorld.ts` because that seam is where the sky's daylight and the world's terrain height are both in hand: `birds.ts`'s flocks (one instanced draw, the flap in the vertex shader, rings re-cut beyond the fog when the walker outruns them), `fauna.ts`'s herd (six CC0 skinned animals, one or two per biome, grazing and bolting, the only downloaded models out here and deliberately never awaited by anything that gates a frame) and `pedestrians.ts`'s crowd (`buildPlayerBody()` rigs walking the sidewalk slab by sampling `roadAt().walk`, so the people in a town are literally the same character every player wears, and a car can knock them flat).

- `src/game/vehicles/` is **the fleet**: a car, a boat and a helicopter, one per medium, so the sea and the mountains are somewhere you can actually get to, and a small ship (`ship.ts`) that flies in the air and in space and is flown through the Earth-Moon seams with its crew aboard (every level that says `vehicles` runs all four, the Moon included, at its own gravity; each machine is in one level at a time, so one left on the Moon is not on the street until it is ordered again). Any empty machine can be taken on the physgun (registry.ts's `pick`/`take`: a Rapier prop stands in for it until it settles upright), with the thrower its single authority on the wire through a server-arbitrated `hand` claim. Models are procedural like everything else. `parts.ts` lofts cross-sections (`ringSuper`'s exponent covers an ellipse, a rounded rectangle and a concave V with one number), and `materials.ts` gives them clearcoat paint reflecting a *painted* equirect env map repainted off the sky's own numbers, because there is no environment to capture. Each machine owns its dynamics; `registry.ts` owns the fleet, the collision bookkeeping, enter/exit and a **fixed 1/120 s substep** (50 ms of explicit Euler through a wheel spring is not a suspension). Three rules bite: a vehicle must never see its own collision box (`supportY` has no owner concept, so it would climb itself, and the registry empties the box before each tick); what a walker collides with is each machine's oriented `hull`, its box being only the broad phase around that (the AABB of a car parked askew is mostly invisible wall, and the helicopter's was an 18-unit rectangle around a cabin with a stick out the back); and the fleet is **session state, not world state**, so nothing is written into the world, and with no server every client's own spawn puts all four back on their probed home spots. The fleet is also the one part of the runtime the shared walk touches: each machine seats two, the server arbitrates the eight chairs and relays the driver's transform, and a machine somebody else is driving is placed by `Vehicle.netStep` rather than integrated (the local physics and the wire would write the same six numbers every frame, and the visible result is a car that shivers). `README.md`'s "The fleet" section is the guide.

## Conventions that matter in here

- **A light's target is a scene object, and the scene freezes matrices.** CrtScene sets `matrixAutoUpdate = false` on everything not marked `userData.dynamic` after the first frame, and a DirectionalLight aims at its `target`'s *matrixWorld*, not its `position`. The sun's target was never marked, so for as long as the sun has followed the player its target stayed at (0, 0, 10): at home the sun shone from where it should, and a few hundred units out it grazed in from the direction of the house (the street at (-32, -331) was lit from ten degrees up), and on the Moon from the horizon. Anything that moves a target every frame marks it dynamic.
- **Hide a subtree's drawables, not its root, if the root carries lights.** A light leaving the scene changes `NUM_POINT_LIGHTS` and relinks every lit program in one frame (17 links, measured, the first time the house was hidden from orbit). CrtScene's orbit hide walks the house for meshes and restores exactly those.
- **A point in front of a body is not a test for whether it can go there.** `fauna.ts` refuses a step by clipping the whole step as a *segment* against each box, and the two versions before it both tunnelled: one point two units ahead steps clean over anything thinner than two units (a garden hedge is forty long and one thick, and deer walked into the same two of them ten times in four minutes), and stepping samples along the segment merely moves the threshold down to the thinnest solid there is, which out here is a house wall. Anything that has to decide *may I move there* — an animal, a pedestrian, a future thrown object — wants the slab clip, which is also cheaper than the five-point version it replaced.
- **A breakable solid you are already touching can never be broken.** `car.ts` compares the car's planar speed against the prop's `Breakable.limit`, and contact removes the closing component of that speed every tick — so a car nosed into a bush with the throttle buried never reaches 0.1 u/s and the bush is a wall forever (measured against the real collision set: an approach at 5 u/s ended in a four-second dead stop with no way out but reverse). The fix is the shove rule: work spent pressing into anything under `SHOVE_MAX` accumulates and takes it out. Gate anything similar the same way, because the *point* of the limit is that a mature broadleaf stops a hatchback, and a rule that lets you lean on one for three seconds throws that away. The companion half is that undergrowth should never have been asking for a run-up at all — `SNAP.bush` is 2.5, not 6.
- **A vaultable obstacle needs headroom in time, not just in height.** A box stops blocking the instant the feet clear its top, so what matters is how long the jump arc spends above it versus how deep the box is. The hop apex is ~2.08 units; the yard fence looked unjumpable at a 1.9-high, 0.8-deep box (a 0.05 s window, two handspans of travel) and works at 1.7 by 0.44. Check both numbers when adding something meant to be climbed over.
- **A verlet contact must move `prev` with the position it corrects.** Position *is* velocity in verlet, so lifting a particle out of the floor without carrying its previous position up hands the body `lift / SUBSTEP` units per second of free upward speed. At 120 Hz a quarter-unit correction is thirty units a second. Worse, a *constrained* pair turns that into a motor: the ground lifts one end, the distance constraint answers by shoving the other end half as far up, and it climbs forever. A felled lamp post went ten units into the air off its own stump, and a broadleaf fourteen. `tumble.ts`'s `land()` is the fix, and the companion rule is that a body must be *born* consistent with its contacts: the ends are spheres of radius `r`, so the near end starts a radius off the ground rather than on it, with the geometry re-based on the same point so nothing visibly moves.
- **Colour jitter must be multiplicative.** `Color.getHSL` reports lightness in the renderer's *linear* working space, not sRGB, so a tree trunk sits at l ≈ 0.04 and an `offsetHSL(_, _, -0.07)` clamps it to pure black. That bug blacked out half of every forest. Scale channels instead.
- **A swept limb needs a parallel-transported frame, not an up-vector one.** `props.ts`'s `sweep()` carries each ring's reference direction forward by projecting the previous one off the new tangent. Rebuilding it per ring from world up (or from a Frenet normal) flips as the limb passes horizontal and twists the tube inside out, precisely at the branch reaching out of the crown, which is the one you are looking at.
- **A foliage card reads as a green plane unless its texture has holes in it.** `makeLeafTexture` scattered 240 ellipses into a disc they covered twice over, so the cluster was ragged at the rim and solid everywhere else: invisible on a small card, and on a big one exactly the flat slab you see hanging off a crown. It is painted, punched open with `destination-out`, then sown with more leaves over the gaps. The companion rule is size: a card past about half its lobe's radius is wider than the lobe and cannot read as anything but a quad. What is *not* fixable this way is the cards that end up edge-on at the crown's silhouette. For any orientation distribution the same fraction contains the view ray, so widening the facing jitter relocates the slivers instead of removing them.
- **Foliage cards are fill cost; trunk soup is not.** A crown's card count is paid over the whole silhouette of a forest, while a trunk is opaque and a few pixels wide. When `crown()` lobed every fork as well as every tip it doubled a broadleaf's cards for a change nobody could see, so the rule when retuning a tree is: card count stays at or below where it was, vertices in `wood()` may grow. Relatedly, a canopy of perfectly horizontal cards (`cards(..., flat)`) has no thickness in silhouette at all, which is what the `tilt` argument is for, and without it the acacia was a few green streaks floating over the savanna.
- **A root flare must stay inside the kit's `solid.r`** or the player clips into it: collision is one cylinder and the flare is the widest part of the tree. Check `r * (1 + flare * (1 - t/0.22)^1.8)` at the station where the spine crosses y = 0, not at the spine's own origin: `wood()` sinks the trunk below the ground on purpose so the swell is buried.
- **A dome shaded from its own vertex position needs real tessellation.** The cloud shell computes noise from `vDir = position`, which the rasterizer interpolates *linearly*, and across a coarse triangle that is a chord rather than an arc, so every cloud cell is sheared to the facet it lands on. On the old 20x12 sphere the artifact was invisible only because the noise was low-frequency enough to hide it; raising the frequency drew straight-edged rectangles of cloud across the sky. It is 64x40 now, which is five thousand triangles on a mesh that never moves and does not touch the fill cost the shader is actually made of.
- **Anything drawn past 430 units is behind the sky.** The domes are transparent-pass meshes with the depth test on, so they cover whatever lies beyond their radius, which nothing did until the far field (`world/farfield.ts`) drew kilometres of it and rendered as empty background. `levels/altitude.ts` grows the lens's far plane and `SkyHandles.setScale` grows the dome with it; anything else that reaches past the ring must go through the same pair.
- **Surfaces out here are computed, not mapped.** Chunk geometry is one merged soup with no usable UVs, so brick, shingles, paving and bark come from `world/surface.ts`, which reads world position and the face normal in the fragment shader off a one-float `aSurf` code per stamp. `applyFixedSurface()` does the same for a whole material (the authored house's walls). Wind and surface share a single `onBeforeCompile` because a material only gets one.
- **Ground colour and vegetation must read the same "how paved is this" number** (`settlements.ts`'s `pavedAt`). When they disagreed, every street in town rendered as bare concrete with bright green tufts standing in it.
- **A site grid's gates are always stricter than they read, so measure them.** `land.ts` says this about the oasis that shipped firing zero times, and `landmarks.ts` re-learned it: the first coast test was one 90-unit water ring, which is a band filter about sixty units wide on the distance to the shore, and a 400-unit site grid lands in it roughly once in a hundred cells. Fifteen lighthouses in a 22 km square is a feature nobody will ever meet. The probe recipe is in the memory note and takes two minutes: copy `world/{noise,land,biomes,settlements,grid}.ts` into a scratch dir, `sed` the imports to `.ts`, run `node probe.ts`. Tally the *per-gate* pass rate, not just the total.
- **Anything that grades the ground must have a bounded, non-binding release.** `landmarks.ts` flattens a pad and eases out of it under a plane climbing at `BATTER`, the same recipe `townGradedHeight` uses, and the sites are gated flat enough that the plane never actually binds. That is the point of it: an eased blend alone reaches the raw ground exactly at the end of its run, and a *clamped* one does not, so if the clamp ever bites you get a scarp at the rim of the pad instead of a bank. Either scale the run with the rise (the town's answer) or gate the rise so the fixed run is always enough (the landmark's).
- **Only a cardinal-facing rectangle may register an AABB.** The collision model is axis-aligned boxes, and a 15-unit barn parked at forty degrees has a box half again as wide as the barn, which the player meets two units off the wall. That is the same bug the vehicles' `hull` exists to fix, and nothing out here is worth a hull. So `structures.ts` snaps the yaw of anything rectangular to a quarter turn and keeps the free yaw only for shapes whose box is a bad fit at every angle anyway: a tower, a silo, a mast, a ring of stones.
- **A body is scaled onto the walker's eye line through one design point, and everything that rides on one is measured in design units.** `buildPlayerBody` draws the character once and scales the group by `eye / DESIGN_LENS`. That point was once the painted eyes (`DESIGN_EYE`, 2.19), so two players met each other's gaze, and it made the bean a giant as tall as a door; it is now the top of the bean (`DESIGN_CROWN`, 2.78), so a bean stands no taller than the lens it looks out of and every first-person view looks slightly down onto other faces, the same for everyone. Whatever the point is, convert between a lens height and the body through `bodyScale`/`DESIGN_LENS` and never through `DESIGN_EYE`, or the seated lens and the body it sits in disagree. The companion rule is that the group is scaled, so anything parented to it (name plate, speaker badge, chat bubble) is in *design* units: a world-unit height written there gets multiplied a second time on its way to the screen. Stack them off `DESIGN_CROWN` rather than off a fraction of the eye height, or the badge floats where the head used to be at whatever size the body happened to be when the number was picked. And a *seated* group's origin is the face, not the soles (`sit()` hangs the fold from the eye so a seat node can be a cockpit lens), so anything reading a body's origin has to know which of the two poses it is in. This body is mostly trunk, so it sits tall: in the fleet's cabins, which were drawn round the long-legged robot before it, each seat node carries a `userData.fit` (default `CABIN_FIT`) and `sit(fit)` folds the body that much smaller about its eye; the car's face sits in its side window (2.10, folded to 0.8) and the helicopter's chairs further forward than the robot's; the helicopter and the boat sit at 1 since the body was scaled onto its crown. Every fleet seat also carries a `userData.room` (the cabin at that seat, in the seat's frame): `sit(fit, passenger, room)` folds a rider too tall for the roof smaller (a tall build, a party hat, the helmet) and the seated tick clamps heads and arms inside it, and `npm run measure -- seats` seats the tallest looks in every chair and counts vertices through the hull or glass (0 everywhere; the ship seats two in tandem because side by side they did not fit). Check any seat change with `npm run shoot -- body:seat` (and the car with `body:car`, whose side tile prints how high a rider reaches).
- **The body is one surface, and three things keep it one.** `bodyShape.ts` writes the bean as a signed distance field and polygonizes it with surface nets, so (1) two parts closer than about two grid cells are *one* part with a web between them: an elbow hanging near the flank or two leg stumps a hair apart fuse, and the web stretches six times over and folds the moment either moves. Keep real gaps at least `2 * BODY_STEP` in the bind pose, which is why the arms are drawn in an A-pose (`ARM_BIND`) whose inverse lives in `bindMatrixWorld`, not in the rig. (2) Linear blend skinning folds any sharp bend, so weights are smoothed over the skin in the junction bands, and the shoulders and hips carry helper bones that take a share of their limb's turn (`HELPERS`, followed in playerBody's `followHelpers`). (3) Anything worn is weighted off the skin under it (the nearest bean vertices), never on its own, or a hood and the neck inside it disagree. `npm run measure -- body folds` counts folded triangles on every filmstrip and `npm run shoot -- body:folds --raw` paints them red, which is the only way to tell a visible crease from one buried in an armpit. And three's `applyBoneTransform` answers in the mesh's own frame (attached bind mode), not the world: a probe comparing it with world-space normals reports half the body folded.

## The world harness

**The open world has its own two-part harness, and anything touching `src/game/world/` should use it instead of booting the site** (which costs a login, a boot, a stand-up glide and a teleport for one look). Both are documented at the top of their own files:

- `npm run shoot -- <target...>` photographs the world. Targets are `landmark:lighthouse`, `landmark:*` (one of each kind, as a contact sheet), `biome:wetland`, `town:downtown`, `home` or a bare `x,z`. It starts its own vite on port 5178, drives headless Chrome over CDP and writes a PNG in about a second a tile. `--eye` frames it from a walker's eye line, `--tod 0.78` shoots at dusk, `--pick x,y` raycasts a pixel and prints what is under it, which for merged chunk geometry is the only handle there is, and `--glb <url>[@dx,dz][:clip][~t][*scale][^yaw]` stands a GLB in every tile with one animation clip frozen at a chosen time, which is how a candidate asset gets judged against the world's own light, fog and scale before any of it is wired into the runtime. Several targets in one call become one image, which is what keeps a visual check from turning into an afternoon.
- `npm run measure -- <kits|chunks|landmarks|smoke>` runs the generator in Node and prints numbers: kit vertex counts and bounding boxes, per-chunk build cost by tier and zone, landmark density and the kind mix, and a several-thousand-chunk build that catches exceptions typecheck cannot. `measure eval <file.js>` runs an arbitrary probe with the whole world pre-imported.

Two rules that keep the harness honest. It renders through `makeChunkMats`, the game's own materials, because a probe that hand-rolls its own has no leaf alpha test and no surface pass and therefore draws every tree as a green slab and every wall as a grey rectangle, which is indistinguishable from a regression that is not there. And it takes its own port and kills only what it spawned: `:5173` belongs to whoever started it.

## The house

**The house is two storeys, and the computer room is upstairs.** The desk scene keeps its x and z and sits one storey up at `houseWorld.ts`'s `UP`, so everything CrtScene measures off the desk and its glass (the intro flight, the parked lens, the stand-up glide, `SPAWN`) moves with it; anything that asks for "the floor here" at the spawn must pass the height it stands at (`spawnY(level, x, z, from)`), or it lands in the living room underneath. `houseWorld` builds both storeys with the same wall/door/window/floor helpers from a storey base, one standable slab between them with the stairwell open, and stairs whose collision boxes overlap to the top of the flight (tiled, a tread seam was a line a point-body walked along through the side of the stairs). The clutter is `levels/houseProps.ts`: kitbashed props stamped into two merged draws (lit, and glowing), and the rooms' lamps as the look's *indoor* pools (a negative radius) rather than as lights, because the house keeps its six PointLights and every one more is a term in every lit program.

**The house is furnished with things that work**, and the split between them is the same one everywhere else follows: `levels/houseWorld.ts` says *which* pieces open, where the cushions are and where the television's glass ended up, and three modules that know nothing about a house do the rest. `levels/fittings.ts` hinges the GLBs' own `doorLeft`/`door`/`washerDoor`/`Desk_Drawer*` nodes (the hinge edge and the swing direction are solved off the placed geometry, never tabulated, so a piece registers in one line) and builds the interior behind each leaf, because these carcasses are shells and a door opening on nothing shows you the back of its own front panel. Its cavities are lit with emissive materials rather than a light, since one more PointLight would relink every material in the room the first time somebody opened the fridge. `player/seating.ts` is sitting down, and is deliberately *not* shaped like the fleet: a sofa does not move, so there is no second tick, only a frozen walk, a lens at cushion height and a clamped head inside the ordinary walk frame. And `components/os/houseTv.ts` is the television, which lives on the React side because its picture is a YouTube iframe: a second CSS3D element in the same scene as the AlejOS screen, behind a second hole punched in the canvas, driven entirely by `postMessage`, with no third-party script and no clickable element, because the walk holds the pointer. What is on it is the `CHANNELS` table in that file and nothing else.

## Cubeland

`levels/cubeland/` is a block world (a map, loaded under the cut's card): `gen.ts` generates it as a pure function of block coordinates, and only edits are stored and sent (`net/remoteBlocks.ts`, `server/src/worldBlocks.js`). Four rules bite. **Block ids are append-only** (`sandbox/blocks.ts`): chunks and the wire store the number, so a new block goes at the end of the table. **Every change goes through the level's `edit`**, never `store.set` alone: that is what re-meshes the chunk (and its neighbour on a rim), refreshes the walker's boxes and fills the outbox. **The walker and the props collide with different shapes**: the walker's are merged, shoulder-padded boxes flagged `walkOnly`, the props' are Rapier voxel colliders (`SandboxGround.extra`), flush with the blocks. And **its own four programs** (terrain, water, the outline, the block in hand) link under its loading card, which `npm run drive -- links` counts apart; nothing in it may link after arrival (`npm run drive -- cubeland` checks, with a biome tour, digging, a blast, the physgun and the step-up hop).

## The shared walk's server-owned systems

Rooms, protection, health, creatures, rounds, blueprints and creative tools each have a section in `README.md`; the rules that bite:

- **Server modules are per room.** A new one is built inside `buildRoom` in `server/src/index.js` and dispatched with `roomOf(ws)?.x.handle(...)`, never created at file scope over the global `worldPlayers` (every room would share it). `level` stays the plain level id on the wire; `ws.world.room` is the room, and `ws.room` is the unrelated chat room.
- **Health and damage are server-authoritative.** Everything that hurts goes through `health.hurt(ws, amount, {by, kind})`; the client never says how much (a landing speed is reported and clamped, a shot must have been paid for). Rounds veto through `health.setGuard`, not by editing damage. A new `kind` needs its line in `sandbox.health.kinds` in both languages.
- **Ownership is a mirror.** The client's `may` (`net/remoteSocial.ts`) only saves a round trip; the server refuses again, and Cubeland corrections carry the server's value (`-1` for "generated"). Owner identity is the account or socket key, not the world id.
- **Creatures: the host simulates, the server referees.** The sim steps only on the host client (a guest's is empty and derives from snapshot rows); the wire kind index is append-only and mirrored in `creatures.js`; read the world through the level's `footing`; the director updates before `sandbox.tick`. Creature cells are declared before the atlas packs.
- **Rounds own their clocks and hooks.** Times are ms-left plus the server's `now` (`untilAt`), never `performance.now()` against a server time; a mode is a `roundModes.js` entry plus a `modes/defs.ts` one (a test compares the tables); pvp, respawn and the guard are set on the room's health scope for the round and restored at cleanup; creatures are held peaceful while one plays. A disguise or ring is a batch proxy, never a plain mesh.
- **Tags and the atlas tile pool.** Paint, lamp state and sign text travel as one validated tag (`sandbox/creative/tags.ts`, mirrored by the server's `cleanTag`); sign text is one of 24 pre-declared atlas cells repainted at runtime, and `cell()` throws after the atlas packs, so cell-declaring modules (`creative/signs.ts`, `creatures/cells.ts`) must import before the first material.
- **Blueprints are versioned by prefix.** A share code is `BP1.` + deflated JSON; a format change is `BP2.` with `BP1.` still decoded. The server's `builds.js:checkCode` duplicates the client's validator on purpose, so change both. Props are never persisted (only Cubeland's blocks are), and a blueprint tint ghost, not a translucent one, keeps paste from linking a program.

## Leaving the planet

The planet **ends upward**: flying high (noclip) bends the far field onto a curve that `world/globe.ts` continues as a painted globe of the same fields, the sky thins to black, the streamed ground dithers out and stops, and past a height the Moon is pinned out there as a body to fly to; coming in, the whole of space swings round you until it is underfoot and the 'moon' level (its own cratered ground, a sixth of the gravity, its own sandbox, the Earth hung in its sky) takes over by a seamless seam, with no card and no teleport, walker and ship carried by one offset. The numbers are `levels/space.ts`, the seams `levels/outsideWorld.ts`; space is scaled down and speed grows with height, and `src/game/README.md`'s "Leaving the planet" bullets are the guide.

## Music

**The walk has a soundtrack, and it is the one place on the site where music is recorded rather than synthesized.** `src/game/music/`: seven short pieces (`score.ts`, one per mood: the house, open country, a town, night, the sea, the air, space) with hand-written themes, arranged fresh on every play by `composer.ts` (voice-led pads, walking bass, B-section melodies built from the theme's own rhythms), played by `sampler.ts` on CC0 orchestral recordings (`public/os/music/`, ~10 MB, fetched per piece and never awaited; `scripts/music-samples.py` rebuilds them and measures every note's real pitch, because the libraries disagree about octave numbering and a hand-tuned kalimba sits forty cents sharp), and conducted by `index.ts`, which reads the mood off plain facts CrtScene already has and **lets the music come and go**: a piece, most of a minute of only `ambience.ts`'s wind and birds, then the next. Synthesized flutes were tried first and rejected for sounding like a synthesizer; do not go back. A change of mood *family* (indoors to outdoors, taking off, reaching space) interrupts a piece, a change within one (field to town, day to night) waits for it to end. `npm run music -- <mood|*>` renders pieces offline through the real mixer to `shots/music/` with their levels in dBFS; the console's `music` command pins a mood in place. The frame loop calls it inside a try, and a fault silences the music rather than the game.
